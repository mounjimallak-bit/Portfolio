Axiora Data

A quality-checked dataset and a statistical analysis of poverty and population across the Souss-Massa region of Morocco, built from 48 tables published by the High Commission for Planning (HCP).

Open the live app View source

Summary

Official statistics for the six territories of Souss-Massa are published in tables that are difficult to compare across years and places. This project transcribed 48 of these tables, checked them for internal consistency, and used them to answer descriptive questions about population, housing, finance and poverty. The main analysis examines changes in poverty rates between 2004 and 2014 across communes and urban centre units.

Boxes marked Mathematical note derive the statistics used in this analysis and test some of its assumptions on the data. Each number is labelled by origin: project result (analyse_pauvrete.py, hcpdata.py), new computation (math_checks_axiora.py), theory or future work.

Research question

Did poverty decline uniformly across the communes and urban centre units of Souss-Massa between 2004 and 2014, and where did it decline least?

The question is descriptive. It asks how the distribution of poverty rates changed and how the change differed between territories. It does not test a causal hypothesis.

Data and method

Source. The rates come from the HCP communal poverty evolution sheets for 2004, 2007 and 2014, which report poverty rates for communes and urban centre units across the six territories. Subtotal rows (urban, rural and total for each territory) are excluded, leaving 182 geographic units. HCP source publications.

Units analysed. The analysis uses 179 geographic units with a rate available in all three years. These consist of 175 communes and four urban centre units (AC). Three additional AC records from Chtouka-Aït Baha have no rate in the 2004 and 2007 sheets and are excluded. Because only units with all three rates are kept, the results describe these 179 geographic units only.

Notation. Let xi, yi and zi be the poverty rates (in percent) of geographic unit i in 2004, 2007 and 2014, for the 179 units. The change for unit i is di = zi - xi, in percentage points.

Two levels of description. Distributions of local rates are summarised by the median, the 25th and 75th percentiles, and the interquartile range IQR = Q3 - Q1; at this level each geographic unit counts once, regardless of its population, because the poverty sheets give no population for communes. Territories are also compared on the rate the HCP publishes for each province or prefecture as a whole (its Ensemble row), which is weighted by population. The two levels answer different questions (how local rates changed, and how poverty changed for the people of a territory) and can rank the territories differently.

No hypothesis test. The 179 units are all the units with a published rate in the three years, not a random sample drawn from a larger set, and neighbouring units are not independent. A p-value or a resampling interval would describe a sampling process that did not take place, so none is reported: the figures below are the observed values for the whole set.

Validation. Before analysis, every table passes an automated check of column lengths, totals and territory names. The check reports no errors on the current dataset (see Data and validation below).

Mathematical noteWeighted and unweighted levels, finite populations, and what the rates estimate· statistics · probability
Two means answer two questions Theory

For the units \(i\) of a territory, with populations \(N_i\) and poverty rates \(p_i\), the share of poor people is the population-weighted mean, while the unit-level summaries count each unit once:

\[ \bar p_w = \frac{\sum_i N_i\,p_i}{\sum_i N_i}, \qquad \tilde p = \operatorname{median}(p_1, \dots, p_n) . \]

They differ whenever poverty is correlated with population size. Project result Agadir-Ida-Ou-Tanane in 2004: unit median 21.4 %, published (weighted) rate 8.9 %. The city of Agadir is a single unit with a rate of 3.7 %, in the urban part that holds 79 % of the prefecture's population (384,987 of 487,954 in the 2004 census).

A weighted mean identifies its weights Theory

If the territory rate is the population-weighted mean of its urban and rural rates, \(E = wU + (1 - w)R\), then the urban population share is identified by the three published rates:

\[ w = \frac{E - R}{U - R} . \]

Rounding of the published rates (to 0.05 points in 2004, 0.005 in 2014) propagates to an interval for \(w\), obtained by evaluating \(w\) at the corners of the rounding box (\(w\) is monotone in each rate).

Description of a finite population Theory

The 179 units are all units with published rates, not a sample: their median and quartiles are population quantities, not estimates with a sampling error, which is why no p-value or resampling interval is given. This does not make them exact. Communal poverty rates are small-area estimates that combine a household survey with census data (Elbers, Lanjouw and Lanjouw, 2003): each \(p_i\) is \(p_i^{\mathrm{true}} + e_i\) with an estimation error \(e_i\) whose variance the published sheets do not report, larger for small communes. The Results note shows one consequence of this error.

The median poverty rate fell from 23.0% to 10.2%, a decrease of 12.8 percentage points, or 55.5% of the 2004 median. The decline was not evenly spread over the period. The median fell by 9.0 points between 2004 and 2007 and by 3.8 points between 2007 and 2014. On the published figures, most of the decrease therefore falls in the first three years, but this depends on the three HCP poverty maps being comparable over time, which was not checked (see Limitations).

The spread between geographic units also narrowed. The interquartile range was 14.3 points in 2004 (17.2% to 31.5%) and 9.7 points in 2014 (5.8% to 15.5%). The interquartile range therefore narrowed, so the middle half of the observed units became more similar. This describes the spread of the central 50% only; it does not describe the range between the lowest and highest units.

Change by geographic unit

171 of the 179 geographic units (95.5%) recorded a lower rate in 2014 than in 2004. The remaining eight recorded an increase, with the largest increase being 4.2 points. The largest decline was 40.4 points. The median change is −12.5 points; half of the units changed by between −17.8 and −7.8 points.

This is a different statistic from the difference between the two medians (23.0% − 10.2% = 12.8 points): the median of unit-level changes depends on how each geographic unit moves, not only on where the central observation sits in each year's distribution.

Mathematical noteRobust summaries, change against initial level, and variation between territories· statistics
Why medians and quartiles Theory

The median and quartiles are order statistics. Their breakdown point is 50 % for the median and 25 % for the quartiles: that share of the units can be moved arbitrarily far before the summary does, against 0 % for the mean, which a single extreme unit can shift. For a normal distribution, IQR \(\approx 1.349\,\sigma\) and the median absolute deviation (MAD) \(\approx 0.674\,\sigma\), so comparing them with the standard deviation is a check of shape.

Change against initial level: regression towards the mean Theory

With \(x_i\) the 2004 rate, \(z_i\) the 2014 rate and \(d_i = z_i - x_i\), the least-squares slope of \(d\) on \(x\) is

\[ b = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(z - x,\, x)}{\operatorname{var}(x)} = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(z, x)}{\operatorname{var}(x)} - 1 = r_{xz}\,\frac{s_z}{s_x} - 1 . \]

It is negative whenever \(r_{xz} s_z \lt s_x\), even if no unit converged. Suppose the true rates did not change and each published rate carried an independent estimation error of variance \(\sigma_e^2\): then \(b = -\sigma_e^2/\operatorname{var}(x) \lt 0\). Units that looked poorest in 2004 would appear to improve most, by measurement error alone (Galton's regression towards the mean).

New computation Here \(\operatorname{var}(x) = 107.1\), \(\operatorname{var}(z) = 47.3\), \(\operatorname{cov}(x, z) = 42.2\), so \(b = 42.2/107.1 - 1 = -0.61\) (Pearson \(r = -0.75\) between change and initial level). The relative change \(d_i/x_i\) is almost unrelated to the initial level (Spearman \(-0.07\); median \(-58\,\%\)). The data are therefore consistent with a roughly proportional decline across levels; the strong negative correlation in points is what proportional decline and regression towards the mean both produce, and must not be read as poorer communes progressing faster. The two explanations cannot be separated without the estimation errors of the poverty map.

How much of the variation lies between territories Theory

The total sum of squares splits into between-territory and within-territory parts, \(\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 = \sum_g n_g(\bar x_g - \bar x)^2 + \sum_g\sum_{i \in g}(x_i - \bar x_g)^2\); the between share \(\eta^2\) is the analogue of an intraclass correlation. New computation \(\eta^2 = 0.16\) in 2004 and 0.25 in 2014: most of the variation is between communes of the same territory, and the territory explains a growing part of it.

Discussion

The results show that poverty declined in 171 of the 179 observed geographic units between 2004 and 2014, but the magnitude of the decline varied substantially across territories. On the published figures, most of the regional decline falls before 2007, a pattern that should be confirmed against the HCP methodology before it is interpreted. Tata retained the highest poverty rate in 2014 on both measures and had the smallest relative decline; in points, its decline is the smallest among units but third smallest once rates are weighted by population. These findings describe where poverty declined and by how much, but do not establish the causes of these territorial differences.

Explaining them would require additional information on local investment, employment and infrastructure, which falls outside the scope of this dataset.

Two features of the analysis limit how far the results can be generalised. First, below the level of the territory only unweighted statistics are possible, because the poverty sheets give no commune populations; the comparison of the two levels above shows that weighting changes the ranking. Second, the unit-level figures exclude three urban centre units for which no rate is published in two of the three years.

Mathematical noteSpatial dependence: Moran's I on the commune map· probability · statistics
Definition Theory

With a spatial weight matrix \(W\) (\(w_{ij} = 1\) if communes \(i\) and \(j\) share a border or a corner, queen contiguity), \(S_0 = \sum_{ij}w_{ij}\) and \(z_i = x_i - \bar x\),

\[ I = \frac{n}{S_0}\,\frac{z^\top W z}{z^\top z}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[I] = -\frac{1}{n - 1}\ \text{ under no spatial association} \]

(Moran, 1950). \(I\) is a weighted correlation between each value and its neighbours' values. Its null distribution is obtained here by permutation: randomly reassigning the observed rates to the communes 9,999 times, which assumes exchangeability under the null and conditions on the map and on \(W\).

Limits. \(I\) depends on the choice of \(W\); it is a global summary and does not locate clusters; the measurement errors of the poverty map, themselves spatially correlated through shared survey strata, may contribute to it. Future work Local indicators of spatial association (local Moran) would locate the clusters, and a spatial regression would be needed before any explanatory model.

Data and validation

The 48 tables are stored as aligned columns in a single data module, each with the publication it comes from, and every table passes an automated check before it is used. The check verifies that columns have equal length; that totals equal the sum of their parts, exactly for counts (urban and rural, men and women, provinces and region); that the tables repeated in the application are identical to their source; and that every province and commune shown on a map reaches exactly one polygon of the official HCP boundary files, inside its own province (a commune name alone is not unique in Morocco). A test script introduces typical transcription errors one at a time and checks that each is reported. On the current data the check reports no errors.

These checks establish internal consistency; agreement with the sources was established by comparing every value taken from the 2016, 2017 and 2018 regional yearbooks with the yearbooks themselves. This comparison also identified three figures that do not add up in the publications: the 2016 yearbook prints 12,984 unemployed aged 35–44 in the region, whereas the urban and rural parts (9,538 + 2,510) and the regional total give 12,048, the value used; and two published totals differ from the sum of their parts by one or two units (first-year enrolment in Tiznit, 2018; investment projects, 2017), which are kept as published. In the ENE labour tables for 2017 to 2020, the 2019 and 2020 values match the HCP's published indicators, except the rates for people with a diploma, which the HCP publishes split into two levels; the 2017 activity rates by setting match the 2017 yearbook; the other 2017 and 2018 values could not be matched to a published table. None of these values is used for any result on this page.

Mathematical noteConsistency checks as linear constraints, and what they can and cannot detect· linear algebra

Theory Stack the cells of a table in a vector \(v\). Each published total gives a linear constraint: urban + rural = total, men + women = total, provinces = region. Together they read \(Av = b\), with one row of \(A\) per constraint. A transcription error \(\delta\) turns \(v\) into \(v + \delta\), and the validator detects it exactly when \(A\delta \ne 0\).

  • An error in a single cell violates every constraint containing that cell; in a two-way table with row and column totals, it violates one row and one column constraint, which also locates it.
  • Undetectable errors are the vectors of the null space of \(A\), of dimension (number of cells) − \(\operatorname{rank}(A)\): for instance two compensating errors in the same row of a table that has only row totals, or a whole table replaced by another consistent one. This is why the validator also compares every copy of a table with its source, value by value, matching copies by structure and year (one of its tests fills a 2018 table with the 2017 figures, which no total would reveal).
  • Rates are not additive: a total rate is a weighted mean, \(E = wU + (1 - w)R\), so it can only be checked with weights. The Data and method note shows that this identity, with census weights, holds within rounding for 10 of the 12 territory–year rates.

Scope and personal contribution

Axiora Data builds on work initiated during a remote internship at the HCP regional directorate for Souss-Massa in 2025. I subsequently extended the project into a broader analytical platform and developed the geographic-unit-level poverty analysis presented in this case study.

My contributions cover four areas:

  • Data harmonisation: transcription of 48 HCP statistical tables into a structured, column-aligned dataset.
  • Data quality assessment: development of automated validation checks and investigation of discrepancies against the source publications.
  • Statistical analysis: descriptive analysis of poverty changes between 2004 and 2014, at the level of geographic units and of population-weighted territory rates.
  • Application development: implementation of an interactive platform for exploring socio-economic indicators, visualising territorial patterns and generating reports.

Population and sectors

These indicators place the poverty analysis in its regional context.

Population grew by 15.2% between the RGPH 2004 and RGPH 2014 censuses, from 2,324,142 to 2,676,847. Growth was concentrated in Inezgane-Aït Melloul (+29.0%) and Agadir Ida Outanane (+23.1%), while Tiznit (−4.5%) and Tata (−3.1%) declined.

Regional bank credit equals 59.9% of deposits (29.70 billion DH of credit against 49.56 billion DH of deposits). Agadir Ida Outanane has the highest ratio, at 80.2%.

Limitations

  • Weighting below the territory level. Unit-level statistics count each commune once. Weighted figures are available only for whole territories (the HCP Ensemble rates); weighting communes would require commune populations compatible with the poverty sheets.
  • Labour tables for 2017 to 2020. Their 2019 and 2020 values match the HCP's published ENE indicators, and the 2017 activity rates by setting match the 2017 yearbook; the other 2017 and 2018 values, and the rates for people with a diploma, could not be matched to a published table. They are not used for any result on this page. Every table taken from the 2016 to 2018 regional yearbooks was compared value by value with the yearbook.
  • Comparability over time. The 2004, 2007 and 2014 rates come from three separate HCP poverty maps. Their comparability was not checked against the HCP methodology notes. Many units fall sharply in 2007 and rise again by 2014, which may reflect a change of method rather than a real change. Every rate used here was compared with the HCP sheets (1,200 values, no difference); extreme 2007 values such as Drargua centre (0.5%) are the published figures.
  • Scope. The analysis covers Souss-Massa only and is descriptive. Vulnerability rates are in the dataset but are not analysed here.
