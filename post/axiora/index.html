Axiora Data

A quality-checked dataset and a commune-level analysis of poverty and population in the Souss-Massa region of Morocco, built from 93 tables published by the High Commission for Planning (HCP).

Open the live app View source

Summary

Official statistics for the six territories of Souss-Massa are published as PDF tables that are hard to compare across years and places. This project transcribed 93 of these tables, checked them for internal consistency, and used them to answer descriptive questions about population, housing, finance and poverty. The main analysis concerns the change in commune poverty rates between 2004 and 2014.

Research question

Did poverty decline uniformly across the communes of Souss-Massa between 2004 and 2014, and where did it decline least?

The question is descriptive. It asks how the distribution of commune poverty rates changed and how the change differed between territories. It does not test a causal hypothesis.

Data and method

Source. The rates come from the HCP communal poverty evolution sheets for 2004, 2007 and 2014, which report the poverty rate for each commune of the six territories. Subtotal rows (urban, rural and total for each territory) are excluded, leaving 182 communes.

Sample. The analysis uses the 179 communes that have a rate in all three years. Three urban centre units (AC) of Chtouka-Aït Baha have no rate in the 2004 or 2014 sheets and are excluded. Because the sample is a complete-case sample, the results describe these 179 communes only.

Notation. Let xi, yi and zi be the poverty rates (in percent) of commune i in 2004, 2007 and 2014, for the 179 communes of the sample. The change for commune i is di = zi - xi, in percentage points.

Descriptive statistics. Distributions are summarised by the median, the 25th and 75th percentiles, and the interquartile range IQR = Q3 - Q1. Each commune counts once, regardless of its population. Population weights are not used because commune-level population series compatible with the poverty observations were not available in the assembled dataset.

Inference. Two hypothesis tests and one confidence interval are reported. (i) A two-sided exact sign test of the hypothesis that a commune is equally likely to decline or increase, applied to the signs of di. (ii) A two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired values (xi, zi), which does not assume normality of the differences. (iii) A 95% confidence interval for the median change, obtained by the percentile bootstrap with 10,000 resamples of the 179 communes and a fixed random seed (42).

Justification of the inference. The 179 communes constitute the complete set of communes with comparable observations in the assembled dataset. The descriptive statistics therefore describe this observed set directly. The inferential procedures are used as model-based assessments of the consistency of the observed changes, under a hypothetical framework in which commune-level changes are treated as independent observations. They are not interpreted as uncertainty arising from sampling a subset of communes from the region. Neighbouring communes are unlikely to be independent, because poverty is spatially clustered, so the p-values probably overstate the precision of the evidence. The percentile bootstrap resamples communes independently for the same reason: its interval ignores spatial dependence and is likely too narrow. The percentile method is preferred because it does not rely on a parametric form for the sampling distribution of the median.

Validation. Before analysis, every table passes an automated check of column lengths, totals and territory names. The check reports no errors on the current dataset (see Data and validation below).

Results

The median commune poverty rate fell from 23.0% to 10.2%, a decrease of 12.8 percentage points, or 55.5% of the 2004 median. The decline was not evenly spread over the period. The median fell by 9.0 points between 2004 and 2007 and by 3.8 points between 2007 and 2014, so most of the decrease occurred in the first three years.

The spread between communes also narrowed. The interquartile range was 14.3 points in 2004 (17.2% to 31.5%) and 9.7 points in 2014 (5.8% to 15.5%). The interquartile range therefore narrowed, so the middle half of the communes became more similar. This describes the spread of the central 50% only; it does not describe the range between the lowest and highest communes.

Change by commune and statistical tests

171 of the 179 communes (95.5%) recorded a lower rate in 2014 than in 2004. The remaining eight recorded an increase, with a largest increase of 4.2 points. The largest decline was 40.4 points. The median change is −12.5 points, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of −14.2 to −11.2 points. This is a different statistic from the difference between the two medians (23.0% − 10.2% = 12.8 points): the median of commune-level changes depends on how each commune moves, not only on where the central commune sits.

The sign test depends only on the direction of change and makes no symmetry assumption. The Wilcoxon test depends on the ranks of the changes, and is read as a test of the median only if the changes are symmetric around it. The changes are mildly skewed (skewness −0.4). This does not establish symmetry, so the Wilcoxon result should be read alongside the sign test. No change is exactly zero, so no observation is dropped. The test was computed with SciPy 1.18.1 (two-sided, scipy.stats.wilcoxon). With 179 observations the p-value uses the normal approximation with a variance adjusted for ties, and without continuity correction; tied absolute changes receive average ranks. The statistic W is the smaller of the sums of positive and negative ranks (124). Neither test says anything about the size of the effect, which is given by the median change and its interval.

Tata shows the smallest decline on both measures. Its median rate fell from 21.2% to 16.6%, and the median of its commune-level changes is −5.0 points, the smallest of the six territories. Discussion

The results show that poverty declined in 171 of the 179 observed communes between 2004 and 2014, but the magnitude of the decline varied substantially across territories. Most of the regional decline occurred before 2007. Tata experienced the smallest median commune-level reduction and retained the highest median poverty rate in 2014. These findings describe where poverty declined and by how much, but do not establish the causes of these territorial differences. Explaining them would require additional information on local investment, employment and infrastructure, which falls outside the scope of this dataset.

Two features of the analysis limit how far the results can be generalised. First, the statistics are unweighted, so a small commune and a large commune count equally; a population-weighted measure could give a different picture. Second, the sample excludes three urban centre units for which no rate is published in two of the three years.

Data and validation

The 93 tables are stored as aligned columns and pass an automated check before they are used. The check verifies that columns have equal length, that urban plus rural equals total and men plus women equals total, that the regional row equals the sum of the provinces (within the larger of one unit or 1% of the compared values), that the copies of each table in the data module and in the application agree, and that every province and commune name used in a map reaches a polygon of the official HCP boundary files. Exact zeros in rate columns are reported as warnings for review rather than treated as missing values.

On the current dataset the check reports no errors and no warnings. It also identified a misaligned row in a 2017 housing table, which was corrected against the yearbook. These checks test internal consistency. They do not verify every transcribed value against its publication, and the 2017 to 2020 labour tables were not checked cell by cell.

Scope and personal contribution

Axiora Data builds on work initiated during a remote internship at the HCP regional directorate for Souss-Massa in 2025. I subsequently extended the project into a broader analytical platform and developed the commune-level poverty analysis presented in this case study.

My contributions cover four areas:

  • Data harmonisation: transcription of 93 HCP statistical tables into a structured, column-aligned dataset.
  • Data quality assessment: development of automated validation checks and investigation of discrepancies against the source publications.
  • Statistical analysis: distributional analysis of commune-level poverty changes between 2004 and 2014, including non-parametric tests and bootstrap estimation.
  • Application development: implementation of an interactive platform for exploring socio-economic indicators, visualising territorial patterns and generating reports.

Population and sectors

These indicators place the poverty analysis in its regional context.

Population grew by 15.2% between the RGPH 2004 and RGPH 2014 censuses, from 2,324,142 to 2,676,847. Growth was concentrated in Inezgane-Aït Melloul (+29.0%) and Agadir Ida Outanane (+23.1%), while Tiznit (−4.5%) and Tata (−3.1%) declined.

Regional bank credit equals 59.9% of deposits (29.70 billion DH of credit against 49.56 billion DH of deposits). Agadir Ida Outanane has the highest ratio, at 80.2%.

Limitations

  • Chtouka-Aït Baha, urban population in 2004. The HCP sheets do not give this figure. The value 39,694 is an inference, chosen so that the province figures sum to the published regional total. It affects the 2004 urban share of the region.
  • Unweighted statistics. Each commune counts once. A population-weighted analysis would require commune-level population series compatible with the poverty observations, which were not available in the assembled dataset.
  • Labour tables for 2017 to 2020. These come from the regional results of the ENE survey and were not checked cell by cell against a single published table. They are not used for any result on this page. Most other numeric tables were cross-checked value by value against their source.
  • Projections. The application also extrapolates trends with linear regression. These extrapolations have no held-out evaluation and are not used here.
  • Scope. The analysis covers Souss-Massa only and is descriptive. Vulnerability rates are in the dataset but are not analysed here.
